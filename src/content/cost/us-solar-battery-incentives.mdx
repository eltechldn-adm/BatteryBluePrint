**Update (August 2026):** The US federal Section 25D Residential Clean Energy Credit was terminated for property placed in service after 31 December 2025. For 2026, residential battery storage is governed by the new technology-neutral Clean Electricity Investment Credit (Section 48E). See the [dedicated ITC update page](/incentives/us-federal-solar-battery-tax-credit-itc) for details.

But beyond the Federal credit, many states and utilities offer "stackable" rebates that can cover another 10-50% of the cost.

This guide explains how to claim these incentives and which states offer the "Hidden Gems" of battery funding.

---

## 1. Federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC)

*   **Status (2026):** Section 25D terminated for installations placed in service after December 31, 2025. The Clean Electricity Investment Credit (Section 48E) now governs 2026 storage.
*   **Value:** **30% of Gross Cost** under Section 48E for qualifying zero-emissions facilities (including residential storage under 1 MW) placed in service in 2026 or later.
*   **For 2025 filers:** If you installed before 1 January 2026 under Section 25D and had insufficient tax liability, unused credit carries forward to future tax years via IRS Form 5695.

### Section 48E Rules for 2026+
Unlike the old Section 25D, the Section 48E credit is a technology-neutral framework. Standalone battery storage qualifies as a zero-emissions facility. The base credit is 30% for projects under 1 MW (which exempts residential batteries from prevailing wage requirements).
- The credit is non-refundable — it cannot exceed your tax liability in any given year, but unused credit carries forward.
- **Do not file Form 5695 claiming a Section 25D credit for a 2026 purchase.** You must file the updated IRS forms for Section 48E.

### Key Rules (2026 - Section 48E)
1.  **Standalone is OK:** A standalone battery charged from the grid qualifies.
2.  **Size Limit:** Must be at least 3 kWh (covers basically all home batteries).
3.  **New Equipment:** Cannot be used cells.

## 2. State-Specific Incentives (The Hidden Gems)

With no federal residential ITC available for 2026 purchases, these state programmes are now the primary source of incentive stacking for new US buyers.

### California: SGIP (Self-Generation Incentive Program)
*   **Status:** Active but funding tiers deplete fast.
*   **Value:** $150 - $1,000 per kWh (varies by budget status).
*   **Standard:** Small rebate (~$150/kWh). Roughly $2,000 off a Powerwall.
*   **Equity / Medical:** If you live in a High Fire Threat District (HFTD) *and* utilize medical equipment (CPAP), "Equity Resiliency" funding can cover nearly **100% of the cost**.

### Maryland: Energy Storage Income Tax Credit
*   **Status:** Active.
*   **Value:** 30% of cost (up to $5,000).
*   **Stacking for 2026 purchases:** The federal Section 25D residential ITC expired for 2026 purchases, but Section 48E applies. The Maryland 30% state credit can be stacked with the new Section 48E federal credit. Confirm stacking rules with a tax professional.
*   **Note:** There is a waiting list/queue as funding is capped annually.

### Massachusetts: ConnectedSolutions
*   **Type:** Performance Payment (Not upfront cash).
*   **Value:** Earn ~$225 - $275 per kW per summer.
*   **Impact:** A standard 2-battery system can earn you **$1,500 per year** just for letting the utility borrow power a few times a summer.
*   **5-Year Value:** ~$7,500.

### Hawaii: Smart DER / Bring Your Own Device (BYOD)
*   **Status:** Replaced the legacy Battery Bonus program.
*   **Value:** Upfront cash incentive based on committed kW + ongoing monthly credits.
*   **Impact:** Substantial incentives to help stabilize the island grid during peak evening hours.

## 3. Utility Virtual Power Plants (VPP)

Utilities like **Duke Energy, Xcel, and Green Mountain Power** are launching VPPs.
*   **Deal:** They give you an upfront discount ($2,000 - $4,000) OR a monthly credit.
*   **Trade:** You give them permission to discharge your battery roughly 20-30 times a year during heatwaves.
*   **Override:** You can usually opt-out if you need the power for a storm.

**Example:** Tesla Electric (Texas) allows Powerwall owners to sell power at massive markups during grid strain automatically.

## 4. Rural Energy for America Program (REAP)

Do you live in a rural area or own a small agricultural business?
The **USDA REAP Grant** is the holy grail.

*   **Value:** Grants for up to **50% of total project cost**.
*   **Eligibility:** Rural small businesses and agricultural producers (farmers).
*   **Stackable:** The 50% REAP Grant can be combined with other applicable state incentives. The federal Section 25D residential ITC expired for 2026 purchases, but Section 48E applies — confirm your installation date and eligibility with a tax professional.
*   **Result:** You could potentially get an 80% discount on your solar + battery system.
*   **Note:** This is competitive and requires paperwork. Hire a grant writer.

## 6. SRECs: The "Solar Coin"

While primarily for solar production, **Solar Renewable Energy Certificates (SRECs)** add income.
In states like NJ, DC, and MD, every MWh your solar produces generates a certificate you can sell for cash ($200+ in DC).
*   **Battery angle:** While batteries themselves don't generate SRECs, they allow you to install larger solar arrays (by preventing clipping/export limits), maximizing your SREC generation.

    <DocsFAQItem question="Did the 30% ITC apply to DIY installs? (2025 filers)">

You cannot sell it to your neighbor. **However**, recent IRA changes made the "Commercial" credit (Section 48) transferable. If you run a business from home and install the battery under a corporate entity, you *might* be able to sell the credit, but the paperwork cost usually outweighs the benefit for small systems. Stick to claiming it personally.
    </DocsFAQItem>

## How to Claim Carry-Forward ITC Credits (2025 Filers Only)

This section applies **only to homeowners who installed before 1 January 2026** and have unused Section 25D credit to carry forward. If you installed in 2026, this section does not apply to you.

### Step 1: Confirm Eligibility

You must:
- Have owned (not leased) the solar and/or battery system
- Have had the system installed at your primary or secondary US residence **before 1 January 2026**
- Have unused credit remaining from a prior qualifying tax year

If you installed in 2026, stop here — no Section 25D credit is available.

### Step 2: Complete IRS Form 5695

The carry-forward credit is claimed on **IRS Form 5695** (Residential Energy Credits). You’ll need:
- The total installed cost of the original qualifying system (from your installer invoice)
- The date the system was placed in service (must be before 1 January 2026)
- The amount of credit generated but not yet used

Your tax professional should confirm the correct carry-forward amount.

### Step 3: Apply the Credit to Your Tax Return

The carried-forward credit reduces your federal income tax liability dollar-for-dollar in the tax year it is applied. The carry-forward continues until fully consumed or until any legislative reinstatement changes the rules.

### Step 4: Keep Your Documentation

Retain all receipts, permits, and installer documentation for at least 3 years in case of an IRS audit.

## Common Questions (FAQ)

### Can I claim the ITC if I have no federal tax liability?
If you owe no federal taxes (e.g., you're retired with only Social Security income), you cannot directly benefit from the ITC. However, the credit carries forward indefinitely until it's used. If you expect to have tax liability in future years, the credit will eventually be applied. Consult a tax professional for your specific situation.

### Does the ITC apply to battery-only installations (no solar)?
As of the end of 2025, the Section 25D residential ITC has expired for new residential installations. Under the Inflation Reduction Act, eligible standalone residential batteries (with a capacity of at least 3 kWh) placed in service before 1 January 2026 qualified for the Section 25D credit without any renewable charging source requirement.

### What is the difference between a tax credit and a tax deduction?
A tax credit reduces your tax bill dollar-for-dollar. A tax deduction reduces your taxable income. For a homeowner in the 22% tax bracket, a $1,000 deduction saves $220, while a $1,000 credit saves $1,000. The ITC is a credit, making it far more valuable than a deduction of the same amount.

### Was the ITC extended? What is its current status?
The Section 25D residential credit was terminated effective January 1, 2026 by H.R. 1 (signed July 2025). It is not currently available for new residential battery installations. For the most current information on what incentives remain, see our [US state rebates guide](/incentives/us-state-solar-battery-rebates-guide-2026).

## Engineering Reality

US battery incentive programmes are legally well-established, but the administrative details of claiming them create outcomes that frequently differ from the headline benefit.

**ITC non-refundability (relevant for 2025 carry-forward filers).** The Section 25D credit is non-refundable and reduces Federal tax liability dollar-for-dollar. For a household with $15,000 installed cost and $4,500 ITC, full utilisation requires $4,500 in Federal tax liability. The median US household pays approximately $8,200 in Federal income tax annually — sufficient to absorb the credit in Year 1. However, a retired household on Social Security with $2,200 in annual Federal tax liability will require 2 years to fully utilise a $4,500 credit. The time-value loss is modest but real, and payback calculations that assume Year 1 full credit utilisation overstate the financial benefit for lower-income or retired purchasers.

**Battery-only ITC claims (2025 filers, carry-forward).** For eligible installations placed in service before 1 January 2026, standalone residential battery storage (minimum 3 kWh capacity) qualified for the Section 25D credit. Unlike previous iterations of the commercial ITC (Section 48), the residential Section 25D credit as amended by the IRA did not impose a renewable charging source requirement for standalone battery storage technology.

**SGIP funding rounds are exhausted within hours or days of opening.** California's Self-Generation Incentive Program is described as offering $150–$1,000/kWh depending on tier. The Equity Resiliency tier (providing near-full system cost coverage for qualifying households in High Fire Threat Districts) is genuinely available but is significantly oversubscribed relative to annual funding allocations. Budget notifications from CPUC indicate remaining Equity Resiliency funds are typically depleted within days of each funding round opening. A homeowner in an eligible area who has not pre-registered their interest is unlikely to capture this specific tier without a multi-year wait.

**VPP programme terms allow unilateral dispatch changes.** Virtual Power Plant programmes that provide upfront discounts in exchange for dispatch rights are correctly described as offering $2,000–$4,000 in upfront value. However, VPP contract terms typically allow the utility to modify the Annual Dispatch Event limit, season timing, and payment structure with 60–90 days notice. A homeowner who modelled backup resilience around 15 annual dispatch events may find the programme terms allow 40 events in a severe heat wave summer, partially compromising backup availability.

## When This Approach Breaks Down

US incentive stacking is frequently presented as an additive calculation. Several programme limitations make the full stack unavailable in specific circumstances.

**High-income tax households with Alternative Minimum Tax exposure (2025 carry-forward filers).** The Section 25D credit is a "below the line" credit that reduces regular tax liability. However, for households subject to the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT), the portion of ITC that reduces regular tax below the tentative minimum tax amount cannot be used in that year — it carries forward. For 2026 AMT exposure is less prevalent than pre-TCJA, but high-income households in high-tax states should confirm with a CPA whether AMT affects their ITC utilisation timeline before counting on full Year 1 absorption.

**Leased systems and the commercial ITC.** For 2026 residential installations, the Section 25D homeowner credit is unavailable. However, homeowners who select a lease or PPA arrangement may see the financing company claim the commercial Investment Tax Credit (Section 48), which remains active. If the lender simultaneously claims a state rebate on the homeowner's behalf, the homeowner may end up with no direct incentive capture while still committed to a 20-year system contract. This is not inherently problematic if the monthly lease payment versus bill reduction calculation is attractive, but the "80% incentive stack" scenario described for REAP-eligible properties requires ownership, not a lease.

**States with income-tested rebates excluding middle-income households.** Some state programmes — particularly Equity-based tiers within SGIP and New York's Empower programme — target low-income households explicitly. Since the Section 25D residential credit is expired for 2026, middle-income homeowners in these states may receive neither a federal credit nor a state supplement, while lower-income neighbours receive state stacking. In markets where the financial case depends on stacking, homeowners above programme income thresholds should model incentives more conservatively.

**Agricultural REAP grant applicants face significant administrative complexity.** The 50% REAP grant for rural agricultural properties is a genuine programme with substantial value. However, REAP grant applications require:
- A completed technical report from a certified energy auditor
- Site-specific energy savings calculations
- Multi-year financial projections
- Compliance with USDA procurement requirements

The preparation cost for a competitive REAP application — including the technical report and grant writing — typically runs $3,000–$8,000. For a 50% grant on a $15,000 system ($7,500 value), the preparation cost erodes most of the grant benefit unless the system is significantly larger (commercial-scale) or the grant writer charges below market.

## Engineering Recommendation

US state and utility incentive programmes collectively represent genuine and substantial financial support for battery investment. A comprehensive incentive stack — combining state rebates, utility BYOD programmes, and VPP income — is achievable for many 2026 purchasers, but requires proactive programme awareness and careful sequence planning.

**Historical Note on the Federal ITC (Pre-2026 installations only):**
- For property placed in service *before* 1 January 2026, homeowners must file IRS Form 5695 correctly in the tax year the system was placed in service. This guidance applies only to the original eligible credit or its carry-forward. It does not apply to a new 2026 installation.
- If your Federal tax liability in 2025 was below the generated ITC credit value, consult a CPA about carry-forward strategy.

**For 2026 programme-specific eligibility verification:**
- SGIP: Check CPUC's SGIP database and your utility's SGIP administrator (PG&E, SCE, or SoCalGas) to confirm current funding tier and waitlist status.
- Massachusetts ConnectedSolutions: Your utility (Eversource, National Grid) administers the programme — contact them directly to confirm your battery qualifies for dispatch participation.
- REAP grant: Contact your local USDA Rural Development state office to confirm eligibility before paying for a technical report.

**The key decision trigger** for 2026 purchases is whether your state offers a programme that meaningfully reduces the gross cost, given the expiration of the federal residential ITC.
