## Methodology and Comparison Criteria

BatteryBlueprint compares residential energy storage systems using manufacturer-reported technical specifications. **We have not physically bench-tested these products.** Our comparison relies on published data sheets, warranty documents, and electrical engineering principles to evaluate:

- **Continuous Output (kW):** The sustained power the battery can provide without thermal throttling.
- **Peak Output (kW):** The surge power available for starting heavy loads (e.g., HVAC compressors).
- **Usable Capacity (kWh):** The actual energy available for discharge, excluding depth-of-discharge buffers.
- **Round-Trip Efficiency:** The manufacturer's reported percentage of energy retained after charging and discharging.
- **Warranty Duration:** The manufacturer's stated term and cycle life.
- **Cost per Usable kWh:** A BatteryBlueprint-derived metric calculating the published MSRP hardware cost divided by the usable capacity.

## Key Specification Definitions

### 1. Continuous Power Output (kW)
Determines instantaneous load capability. 
*   **5 kW:** Sufficient for standard 120V household loads (refrigerators, lighting, electronics).
*   **11.5 kW+:** Required for starting and running heavy 240V loads like central air conditioning or electric ranges without grid support.

### 2. Usable Capacity (kWh)
Determines duration of backup or arbitrage operation.
*   **5-10 kWh:** Sufficient for evening time-of-use shifting or short-duration outages.
*   **20-30 kWh (Multiple Units):** Required for true 24-hour whole-home backup during extended grid failures.

### 3. Chemistry
*   **LFP (Lithium Iron Phosphate):** The current industry standard. Features 6,000+ cycle life and excellent thermal stability. All four batteries in this comparison utilize LFP cells.
*   **NMC (Nickel Manganese Cobalt):** Higher energy density but lower cycle life and higher thermal runaway risk. Largely phased out of new residential stationary storage designs.

## Engineering Reality

Battery brand comparisons on specification sheets represent controlled-condition best-case performance. Several factors cause real-world systems to diverge from the datasheet.

**Round-trip efficiency figures are measured under specific test conditions.** The GivEnergy AIO is rated at 88% round-trip efficiency — lower than the Enphase 5P's 96%. However, the GivEnergy's lower figure is measured at a full 6 kW discharge rate. At typical household discharge rates (1–2 kW), the measured efficiency often improves. Both figures narrow in practice.

**11.5 kW continuous output from the Powerwall 3 is conditional.** Tesla specifies 11.5 kW continuous subject to thermal conditions. In sustained high-load operation (e.g., running a 3-ton heat pump and EV charger simultaneously over several hours), the Powerwall 3's thermal management system will throttle output to protect cell integrity. This is correct engineering behaviour, but users should understand that peak output is not infinite-duration output.

**Software ecosystems lock you into one manufacturer's cloud.** Tesla's energy management software is excellent for US TOU markets. However, the Powerwall ecosystem does not provide an open API. Third-party home energy management platforms cannot directly query Powerwall data. If you intend to use a third-party aggregator, confirm that your battery's API is accessible before purchasing.

**Warranty depth matters as much as warranty length.** FranklinWH's 12-year warranty and Enphase's 15-year warranty both exceed the industry-standard 10 years. However, warranty coverage terms differ significantly regarding capacity retention guarantees and labor reimbursement. Read the warranty document — not the marketing summary — before making a final comparison.

## Real-World Financial Example

**Scenario:** A homeowner in San Jose, California purchases a Tesla Powerwall 3 in February 2026, motivated by California NEM 3.0 economics — where solar export rates have dropped to approximately $0.05/kWh, and import rates at peak reach $0.55/kWh. Their goal is to arbitrage the $0.50/kWh spread through daily cycling.

**First 6 months of operation:**
- Daily cycles: 365 per year at 13.5 kWh (full cycles)
- Peak-rate imports displaced: 1,820 kWh (saving $1,001 at $0.55/kWh)
- Off-peak grid charging cost (where solar insufficient): $183
- PG&E demand charge triggered: 3 times (Powerwall's 11.5 kW output caused brief demand peaks on a commercial-adjacent tariff — unexpected and worth $47 in penalties)
- **Net year-1 financial benefit: $771**

At $14,000 gross installed cost (note: the Section 25D federal residential credit expired for 2026 purchases), the payback period is approximately 18 years — longer than many initial estimates because off-peak top-up costs and demand charges were not modelled at point of sale.

**Lesson:** California NEM 3.0 arbitrage economics are real but sensitive to accurate modelling.
