**Critical update — August 2026:** The US federal Section 25D Residential Clean Energy Credit (the 30% battery and solar tax credit) was terminated for property placed in service after 31 December 2025. This page explains the current status, what options remain, and what 2025 filers with carry-forward credits need to know.

*Primary source: IRS guidance on Section 25D; H.R. 1 ("One Big Beautiful Bill"), signed July 2025. Reviewed August 2026.*

## What Happened to the 30% Federal ITC?

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 had legislated the residential Section 25D credit at 30% through 2032. That schedule was overridden by H.R. 1, signed into law in July 2025 ("One Big Beautiful Bill"), which terminated the residential credit for any qualified clean energy property **placed in service after 31 December 2025**.

There is **no phase-down** — the credit went from 30% to 0% for 2026 installations without the 26%/22% step-down schedule that was previously legislated.

**For homeowners installing in 2026 or later:** No federal residential tax credit is available under Section 25D.

**For homeowners who installed in 2025 or earlier:** If you have unused Section 25D credit from an eligible installation completed on or before 31 December 2025, you can still carry that credit forward to offset federal tax liability in future tax years.

## What Federal Options Exist for Battery Storage in 2026?

### Section 48E — Commercial and Third-Party Ownership

The Section 48E Clean Electricity Investment Credit may be available to qualifying commercial and third-party owners. Eligibility depends on ownership, project structure, equipment, timing, tax status, and current law. See [IRS guidance on Section 48E](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/clean-electricity-investment-credit-section-48e). This means:

- If you **lease** a battery or enter a **PPA (power purchase agreement)**, the financing company — not you — may claim Section 48E on the system.
- You do not receive a direct tax credit in this scenario, but you may benefit indirectly through lower monthly lease payments if the provider passes through some of the credit value.
- This is a commercial credit with different eligibility rules. Confirm with any third-party provider whether and how they pass through this benefit.

### State-Level Incentives

Several states maintain active battery storage incentive programmes that do not depend on the federal Section 25D credit:

**California — SGIP (Self-Generation Incentive Program):**
Availability varies by SGIP budget category and programme administrator. Some budgets are exhausted or waitlisted. Check current [SGIP Program Metrics](https://www.selfgenca.com/home/program_metrics) and waitlists before relying on an incentive.

**New York — NY-Sun Storage Incentive:**
Available through NYSERDA. Amounts vary by utility territory ($250–$400/kWh). Check [nyserda.ny.gov](https://www.nyserda.ny.gov) for current status.

**Massachusetts — SMART Programme:**
Performance-based incentive for solar+storage. Check with your utility (Eversource or National Grid) for current capacity and eligibility.

**Hawaii — State Tax Credit:**
Hawaii's state 35% tax credit (capped at $5,000) is a state-level instrument and is not affected by the federal Section 25D termination. Confirm current status at the Hawaii Department of Taxation.

**DSIRE Database:** The most comprehensive source for current state, utility, and local incentive availability is [dsireusa.org](https://www.dsireusa.org/).

## For 2025 Filers: Claiming a Carry-Forward Credit

If your battery system was placed in service on or before 31 December 2025, you may have an eligible Section 25D credit. Under IRS rules, unused Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) from an eligible prior year may still be carried forward to the succeeding tax year. (Note: This carry-forward ability is unique to Section 25D and does not apply to the separate Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit under Section 25C).

**Carry-forward mechanics:**
- The residential Section 25D credit is non-refundable — it offsets federal income tax liability to zero but does not generate a refund.
- If your 2025 credit exceeded your 2025 federal tax liability, the unused portion carries forward to 2026.
- The carry-forward can continue year-to-year until the credit is fully used.

**Documentation to retain (IRS audit window: 7 years):**
- Final itemised installation invoice showing all costs
- Proof of installation date (permit sign-off, utility interconnection date, or commissioning documentation)
- IRS Form 5695 from the filing year
- Copy of your tax return showing credit applied

**Common 2025 carry-forward errors:**
1. **ITC basis overclaim:** Only costs directly attributable to the eligible battery system qualify. Main panel upgrades not required by the battery installation, non-electrical permitting fees, and homeowner-supplied components are typically excluded. An incorrectly inflated basis generates IRS assessment risk.
2. **Failure to reduce basis by state rebates:** Utility and state rebates that reduced your installation cost must be subtracted from the ITC basis before calculating the 30% credit. California SGIP rebates, for example, reduce the ITC basis directly.
3. **Assuming full Year 1 utilisation:** If your 2025 federal tax liability was below the credit value, only the amount equal to your tax liability was used in 2025. The remainder carries forward.

If you are uncertain about the correct basis calculation for a 2025 installation, consult a qualified tax professional before filing your 2025 or 2026 carry-forward return.

## What Does This Mean for Battery Purchase Economics in 2026?

Without the 30% federal credit, the economics of residential battery storage in the US change materially for 2026 buyers.

**Historical example (2025 purchase, credit was available):**
- Gross installed cost: $14,000
- 30% Section 25D credit: -$4,200
- Net cost: **$9,800**
- Payback at $1,800/year savings: approximately 5.4 years

**Current example (2026 purchase, no federal credit):**
- Gross installed cost: $14,000
- No federal residential credit available
- Net cost: **$14,000**
- Payback at $1,800/year savings: approximately 7.8 years

The difference is approximately 2.5 years of additional payback for the same system in the same state. In high-rate states (California, New York, Massachusetts) with strong state incentives and time-of-use tariff arbitrage, battery storage remains financially viable. In low-rate flat-tariff states without state-level support, the financial case is significantly weaker without the federal credit.

Hardware prices for residential batteries have continued to decline — approximately 15–20% between 2023 and 2026 — which partially offsets the loss of the federal credit, particularly for value-tier systems.

## Regional Impact Assessment: Which States Are Most Affected?

The impact of the Section 25D termination varies significantly by state, primarily because state-level incentives and electricity rate structures differ so much.

**States least affected by the credit termination:**

**California** — Despite the Section 25D expiry, SGIP Equity Resiliency still provides near-full coverage for eligible households (fire zone, low income, medical needs). For standard-tier applicants, SGIP waitlists are long, but the combination of California's high TOU rates ($0.40–$0.60/kWh peak on SCE and PG&E tariffs) and NEM 3.0 battery incentives mean battery payback remains competitive at 6–9 years even without federal credits.

**New York** — The NY-Sun Storage Incentive ($250–$400/kWh depending on utility territory) provides a meaningful state-level buffer. Combined with NY's high electricity rates and the NYS 25% state tax credit (up to $5,000), the effective incentive stacking in New York remains significant.

**Massachusetts** — The SMART programme's storage incentive, combined with the state's high electricity rates and net metering structure, means Massachusetts battery economics remain among the best in the country without the federal credit.

**Hawaii** — The state's 35% tax credit (capped at $5,000) and very high electricity rates ($0.35–$0.45/kWh) make battery storage financially attractive without any federal credit.

**States most affected by the credit termination:**

**Texas** — No state battery incentive, moderate electricity rates ($0.12–$0.18/kWh), and TOU adoption is growing but not universal. The loss of the 30% federal credit significantly extends payback for Texas homeowners without state-level offset. Battery investments in Texas are increasingly driven by resilience value (grid failures, freeze events) rather than financial return.

**Midwest states (Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Wisconsin)** — Cheap grid power ($0.10–$0.14/kWh), no state battery incentives, and rare outage events meant battery payback was already marginal at 12–18 years with the federal credit. Without it, pure financial returns are difficult to justify in most Midwest markets.

**Florida** — Florida's net metering rules were changed in 2023, and battery incentives are limited at the state level. The loss of the federal credit makes battery storage in Florida primarily a resilience and hurricane backup investment rather than a financial one.

**Southeast states (Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi)** — Low electricity rates and no state incentives. Battery storage in these markets was barely viable with the federal credit and requires significant backup resilience justification without it.

## How to Evaluate Battery Economics Without the Federal Credit

For homeowners considering battery storage in 2026, the financial analysis requires a more careful regional approach now that the federal credit is unavailable.

**Step 1: Confirm state and utility incentives**
Use the [DSIRE database](https://www.dsireusa.org/) to check every state, utility, and local incentive available at your address. Many homeowners are unaware of utility-specific rebates ($500–$2,000 from utilities like Xcel Energy, Evergy, and smaller cooperatives).

**Step 2: Calculate your actual TOU savings**
Battery payback is determined primarily by the spread between off-peak and peak electricity rates on your tariff. If you're on a flat rate, the battery's financial value is primarily self-consumption savings — charge when solar generates, discharge at night. If you can access a time-of-use tariff (charging at $0.07/kWh off-peak, avoiding $0.45/kWh peak imports), the arbitrage value is the dominant return driver.

**Step 3: Value backup honestly**
Without the federal credit reducing upfront cost, backup power capability becomes a more significant part of the total value proposition. A battery that provides 8–12 hours of whole-home backup during grid outages has an insurance value that may justify the investment even in markets where financial payback is borderline.

**Step 4: Compare with rising hardware efficiency**
Battery hardware costs have fallen significantly. Where a 13.5 kWh Powerwall installation cost $15,000–$17,000 in 2023, the same capacity is available at $12,000–$14,000 installed from competitive installers in 2026. Lower hardware costs partially compensate for the absent federal credit.

**Step 5: Check lease/PPA terms for Section 48E pass-through**
Third-party ownership models (leases and PPAs) may pass through some value from the Section 48E credit, if the third-party owner qualifies. You, as the homeowner, do not directly receive the Section 48E credit when a third party owns the system. Any savings passed through to you depend entirely on your contractual terms. Ask providers for clear documentation of these terms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I claim the federal ITC if I install a battery in 2026?**
No. The Section 25D residential credit does not apply to systems placed in service after 31 December 2025.

**Does the credit apply if I signed a contract in 2025 but installed in 2026?**
The determining date is when the system is placed in service (fully installed and operational), not when the contract was signed or paid. If installation was completed in January 2026 or later, the credit does not apply.

**Can I claim the credit on a battery added to an existing solar system in 2026?**
No — the Section 25D credit applies to property placed in service before 1 January 2026.

**What if I lease my battery from a provider?**
The leasing company may be eligible for the Section 48E commercial credit, depending on current tax law and their specific project structure. You do not directly receive a tax credit. Confirm all savings and terms with your specific provider.

**Is the Section 25D credit likely to be reinstated?**
This cannot be predicted. The credit was reinstated after previous expirations. There is no current active legislation to restore it for residential purchasers. Do not make purchase decisions based on anticipated reinstatement.
